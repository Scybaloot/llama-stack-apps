Super Dario Bros.

A webcam pose-controlled 8-bit platformer. Raise your arms and Dario jumps. Punch them forward and he throws a Claude Code sprite. The character isn't a puppet you operate — he's a mirror.

01 · THE CENTRAL QUESTION

What does the camera view actually look like?

This is the question you flagged, and it's the right one to settle first — everything else follows from it. Three options:

A

AR overlay — you're composited into the level

Rejected. Your body lives in camera-space and the level lives in game-space; there's no honest way to make your real knee land on a virtual platform. This is a Kinect party game, not a Mario game.

B

Side-scroller with a webcam picture-in-picture

Classic side view, small camera panel in the corner. Safe, readable, and a little inert.

C

Side-scroller where the character is the mirror

No separate feed. Dario's limbs are continuously driven by yours. Your arms go up, his arms go up, and the jump happens.

RECOMMENDATION

C as the game, B as the instrument panel. The side view was your instinct and it's correct. A character that live-puppets your body is what makes it feel like you're Dario rather than you operating a remote control — the feedback loop becomes proprioceptive. You don't check a UI to learn whether the gesture registered; you look at whether the little guy's arms are up.

Keep the PiP anyway, for one reason: when a gesture doesn't register, the player needs to know why. Small, cornered, showing your silhouette with the tracked skeleton over it and a framing guide box. It answers "am I in frame, is the room too dark" without pulling focus — and during development it's your entire debugging surface.

So: full side-scrolling stage, Dario puppeted by your pose, skeleton PiP bottom-right, and two indicator lamps (JUMP / THROW) that flash on the rising edge so you can see the system's read of you in real time.

02 · ARCHITECTURE

Separate "pose → intent" from "intent → physics"

This is the decision that matters most. One module reads camera frames and emits an intent struct, and does nothing else:

{ jump: false, throw: false, lean: -0.2, crouch: false,
  armAngleL: 1.2, armAngleR: 1.1, tracking: 'good' }

The game reads that struct at 60Hz and never sees a landmark. Three things fall out for free:

  • Keyboard input is always live. It writes the same struct. You can build and test the whole game with no camera attached, and anyone can play sitting down.
  • The game becomes testable. Feed recorded intent traces, assert on outcomes.
  • The pose model is swappable without touching game code.

Run the two on separate clocks. Pose inference gets its own loop capped near 30fps; the game runs a fixed 60Hz timestep and reads the most recent intent. If inference stutters, the game keeps animating smoothly instead of hitching.

Gesture vocabulary

Why auto-run

You said "you naturally are moving forward," and auto-run is the right reading of that. Continuous horizontal control from body position is jittery, tiring, and competes for the same body signal the jump needs. Locking the run frees your arms for the two things that matter. Lean-to-modulate-speed is a good addition once the core feels solid.

Why jump is an edge, not a hold

Holding your arms overhead between jumps is exhausting inside of a minute. Jump fires on the transition into arms-up and your arms come straight back down.

This costs us the reference game's variable jump height, which comes from holding the button through the ascent. v1 accepts that and ships a single fixed arc — see the decisions section. If it is added later, the mechanism is holding your arms up during the ascent only, roughly 300ms, which the edge trigger already permits and which fatigue does not argue against.

Four signal-processing rules, or it will feel broken

  • Smoothing. One-Euro filter on landmarks — low-pass but latency-aware. A plain moving average adds enough delay to make jumps feel mushy.
  • Hysteresis. Dual thresholds: fire at 1.05× shoulder height, don't re-arm until wrists drop below 0.95×. Kills chatter at the boundary.
  • Refractory period. Roughly 250ms cooldown on throw, so one arm motion isn't read as four.
  • Confidence gating. If landmark visibility drops, freeze the last good intent and surface "step back into frame" in the PiP. Never let a low-confidence frame trigger an action.

Calibration

Three seconds of standing still at the start of a run captures shoulder width, shoulder height, and head height. Show a live countdown with the skeleton overlay so the player can see it working. Offer a recalibrate key, and a seated profile for players who can't stand.

04 · CONSEQUENCE

Latency changes the level design

A gesture-driven jump lands 100–200ms later than a keypress: model inference, plus filter delay, plus your own body's travel time. That's not a bug to optimize away — it's a constraint to design around. The course has to be built for a body, not for thumbs.

  • Wide, forgiving landing platforms
  • Slower scroll speed than Mario
  • Generous coyote time — ~150ms of grace after leaving a ledge
  • Jump buffering — ~200ms, so a jump registered just before landing still fires
  • Enemy contact costs a size tier, not a life
  • Two checkpoints in a 90-second course

Build 1-1 to be readable at a glance and hard to fail by accident. The challenge should come from performing the gesture, never from parsing the level.

BUILD THE LEVEL AROUND THIS ONE

The power-up is the tutorial. Acquisition follows convention — the item pops out of a bump block, lands, and you pick it up by running into it. No new gesture, nothing to explain. What gets taught is the throw itself, and it's taught by failing.

WhenArms out doesWhat the player sees
Before the power-upNothingThe full throw animation — windup, arms punch out — and nothing comes out. Dario looks at his own empty hands
After the power-upFiresIdentical motion, sprite flies

Why the locked throw plays the real animation

A substitute animation — a shrug, a head-shake — reads as the game didn't understand you. The full throw with an empty result reads as the game understood perfectly; Dario can't do it yet. The failure becomes the character's limitation rather than the system's: diegetic, and funny instead of broken.

It also drills the motor pattern. Every failed attempt is a rep, so the first real throw is actually the player's tenth throw — and the first one that works.

  • The confused beat has to diminish. Full reaction on the first two attempts, smaller for the next few, then just the animation. Twenty identical confused looks is grating.
  • Light the HUD's THROW lamp even while locked. Dario reacting already implies "input received," but the lamp makes it explicit. No player should conclude their camera is broken when the game is only saying not yet.

Aerial throws still work — and this is why the edge-triggered jump was the right call. Arms drop back to neutral immediately after the flick, so you can jump and then punch out mid-air. A hold-to-jump design would have made the two gestures genuinely exclusive.

One Dario, not brothers. Single player. Two-player couch mode — two people in frame, the pose model tracks both — is a genuinely easy stretch goal, but it stays a stretch goal.

Why a blocker and not a hazard

The first thing that demands a jump is a wall, not a pit. Fail at a pit and you die; fail at a wall and you stand there. The player gets to be confused for as long as they need, at no cost, until they work it out.

Auto-run makes this work better here than it does in Mario. Dario hits the rack and runs in place against it forever — a patient, wordless prompt that never times out. It also becomes the game's universal failure mode: a confused player ends up stalled against something rather than dead.

Build in this order too

M1 ships beats 1–4 only: auto-run, two racks, one enemy. That is enough to tell you whether jumping feels good, which is the question that decides the project. Beats 5–6 come next, then 7–9. Don't build the pit until the jump is proven.

07 · STACK

Browser, no server

Everything runs client-side and the camera feed never leaves the machine — worth stating out loud on the title screen.

  • Pose: MediaPipe Tasks Vision PoseLandmarker, GPU delegate, VIDEO mode. 33 landmarks, 30–60fps on a laptop. Alternative: TF.js MoveNet Lightning.
  • Rendering: hand-rolled Canvas2D, fixed timestep, AABB collision. A 2D platformer is well-trodden ground and the whole game is roughly 800 lines. Kaplay is the escape hatch if the camera work eats more time than expected.
  • Sprites as source code. Pixel art authored as arrays of strings with a palette lookup, baked to offscreen canvases at load. No binary assets, fully diffable in git, trivially editable. The sprite at the top of this page is drawn that way.
  • Audio: WebAudio square-wave blips generated in code. No asset licensing, and correctly 8-bit.
  • Build: Vite. getUserMedia needs a secure context — localhost is fine, but demoing from another device needs a cert or a tunnel.

Jump height: fixed for v1

v1 ships a single fixed jump arc, with no height modulation.

The reference game has one jump with continuously variable height, driven by how long the button is held: gravity is reduced while the button is down and the character is still rising, and releasing it cuts the arc short. Players experience "small jump versus big jump," but it is really one action sampled along a range.

We ship fixed anyway, because our input is already noisy and roughly 150ms late. Add height modulation on top and a missed jump has three possible causes — bad gesture, bad timing, bad arm speed — and the player cannot tell which. One jump means one thing to blame. It also makes reachability a constant for level design: with a single arc, "can the player reach it" stops being a question, which matters most at the beat-5 bump block, where an unreachable block costs the player the entire second verb.

If we add it later, use the hold model, not arm speed

An earlier draft of this plan proposed deriving height from arm-raise velocity, sampled at the instant the gesture fires. That is the worse option: beginners raise their arms slowly, so a tentative first-timer gets a weak jump — the game punishing hesitation at exactly the moment the player is least equipped to understand why.

The better mapping is the reference game's own: keep reading arm position during the ascent. Arms still up while rising means a full jump; arms coming down early cuts it short, exactly as releasing the button does. Nothing new to detect — arm position is already sampled every frame.

  • The fatigue objection doesn't apply. The hold lasts only the length of the ascent, roughly 300ms — not continuously between jumps.
  • It fails in the right direction. A player who does nothing deliberate still has their arms up when the ascent ends, so the default is a full jump and the short hop is the deliberate act. That's the correct default; most jumps in the course want full height.
  • Expect low resolution. Arms drop in 200–300ms against a ~300ms ascent, so in practice this may yield only two outcomes: a flick for a hop, anything else for a full jump. Which is fine — two is what players think they have.

M0 decides. Log arm position through the ascent across a few dozen jumps and see whether release timing separates cleanly. If it does, add the hold model after M1. If it doesn't, fixed height is the answer permanently.

What "demo first" defers

Because the target is a room with you standing in it, three things get deferred rather than cut:

  • Camera-permission priming — a cold-load player needs to be told why the browser is about to ask for their webcam. You can just say it out loud.
  • Framing and lighting tutorial — replaced by you pointing at the floor and saying "stand about there."
  • Per-body threshold auto-tuning — v1 ships one sensitivity slider instead of adapting to each player.

All three sit behind the same M5 gate. If three strangers can play it without coaching, harden it into a link. If they can't, the problem is the gestures rather than the onboarding — and that is worth knowing before spending four days on polish.
